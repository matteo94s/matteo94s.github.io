A practical onboarding guide for DTU students starting an MSc thesis or special course, covering project registration, research proposals, literature reviews, resources, meetings, and final report expectations.

Starting an MSc thesis or special course is an opportunity to explore a research topic in depth, develop independent research skills, and apply knowledge acquired throughout your studies. Successful research also requires careful planning, scientific curiosity, effective communication, and a systematic approach.

“The objective of the master’s thesis or special course is to give students the opportunity to apply the knowledge they have acquired in an independent way on a larger project that concludes with a written report.

The final manuscript (thesis or equivalent) must document skills in applying scientific theories and methodologies to a clearly defined academic topic. Students are expected to take full ownership of their project while receiving guidance from a professor and teaching assistants.

Thus, students must organize their work effectively by planning their research, managing their time, and structuring their thesis systematically. Taking ownership of the project involves setting clear goals, following a research methodology, and ensuring steady progress while seeking guidance from the professor and teaching assistant when needed.”

Extended quote from my mentor: Jakob E. Bardram.

1. Before You Begin: Project Registration#

Before starting, agree on the necessary administrative information with your supervisor.

  • Confirm the project start date and end date.
  • Confirm the number of ECTS credits assigned to the project.
  • Agree on the evaluation method (Danish 7-point grading scale or pass/fail, as applicable).
  • Prepare the project description in Danish + English. YES… A danish version of your project description is required!.
  • Confirm the project scope, objectives, and expected deliverables.
  • Complete the relevant DTU registration procedures.

2. How to Write an MSc Thesis or Special Course Proposal#

A research proposal clarifies what you intend to investigate, why the problem matters, and how you plan to address it. Consider:

  1. Research background: What is the topic, and why is it important?
  2. State of the art: What are the most relevant developments in the field?
  3. Research gap: What limitations or unanswered questions exist in the literature?
  4. Research methods: Which methodologies are commonly used, and can they be improved?
  5. Research questions: What three focused questions will you investigate?
  6. Expected contribution: What insights or improvements might your project provide?

Expected length: Max 1 page, excluding references.

3. Onboarding Checklist: Before Your Project Starts#

Computing and research environment#

  • Obtain access to DTU High Performance Computing (HPC) resources, if needed.
  • Create a shared project folder and provide access to saima@dtu.dk.
  • Organize folders for datasets, code, experiments, results, and documentation.

Report preparation#

  • Download the DTU LaTeX report template.
  • Create an Overleaf project using the template.
  • Download the DTU PowerPoint presentation template.
  • Install Zotero to manage scientific references.
  • Connect Zotero to your writing workflow, including Overleaf where appropriate.

Communication and supervision#

  • Schedule a recurring weekly meeting with your principal supervisor (often Sada) and me.
  • Check our Outlook calendars for a suitable time.
  • Establish a shared location for meeting notes and project updates.

Recommendation: Begin writing the report early. Document your methods, experiments, and observations as you go.

4. Preliminary Reading:#

Select the books and chapters most relevant to your research topic; you are not expected to read everything.

Signal processing and biomedical signal analysis#

  • Applied Signal Processing — S. Puthusserypady.
  • EEG Signal Processing and Feature Extraction — Li Hu and Zhiguo Zhang.
  • Bioelectrical Signal Processing in Cardiac and Neurological Applications — Leif Sörnmo and Pablo Laguna.

Machine learning and deep learning#

  • Deep Learning — Ian Goodfellow, Yoshua Bengio, and Aaron Courville.
  • Hands-On Machine Learning with Scikit-Learn, Keras, and TensorFlow (2nd Edition) — Aurélien Géron.

Python and scientific computing#

  • Python for Data Analysis (3rd Edition) — Wes McKinney.
  • Python Data Science Handbook: Essential Tools for Working with Data — Jake VanderPlas.
  • Neural Data Science in Python — Online course.
  • Cursera

5. Your First Week: Getting Started with Research#

Task 1: Understand your research topic#

  • Learn the main concepts and terminology.
  • Identify the scientific problem and why it matters.
  • Discuss the initial research direction with your supervisor.

Task 2: Conduct an initial literature review#

Find state-of-the-art publications using:

  • Google Scholar
  • PubMed
  • IEEE Xplore
  • Scopus
  • DTU Findit

Task 3: Identify relevant datasets#

Useful repositories include:

  • PhysioNet — Physiological and clinical signals.
  • Google Dataset Search — Datasets across disciplines.
  • Kaggle Datasets — Machine learning and data science datasets.

Task 4: Prepare a project timeline#

  • Divide the project into manageable tasks.
  • Define milestones and deliverables.
  • Prepare a Gantt chart.
  • Reserve time for experiments, analysis, writing, and revisions.
  • Review the timeline with your supervisor.
PhaseMain activitiesDeliverable
PreparationLiterature review and research questionsResearch proposal
Data explorationDataset identification and preprocessingInitial data analysis
Method developmentBaselines and proposed methodsWorking implementation
ExperimentsEvaluation and validationExperimental results
AnalysisInterpretation and limitationsResults and discussion
ReportingWriting and revisionFinal thesis or report

Adapt the timeline to the project’s duration, ECTS workload, and requirements.

6. Weekly Supervision Meetings: The 3-P Principle#

Weekly meetings help review progress, address challenges, and set priorities. Prepare a short presentation using the 3-P principle: Progress, Problems, and Plans.

Progress#

What have you accomplished since the last meeting? Summarize completed reading, implementations, analyses, or results.

Problems#

What difficulties or uncertainties have you encountered? Discuss data issues, methodological challenges, unexpected results, and decisions requiring feedback.

Plans#

What will you complete before the next meeting? Define concrete tasks and expected outcomes.

ProgressProblemsPlans
Implemented baseline algorithmLow classification performanceInvestigate feature selection
Reviewed five papersUnclear evaluation protocolDefine validation strategy
Completed preprocessingMissing dataTest data-quality procedures

Remember: Negative findings and failed experiments are valuable when systematically analyzed and communicated.

7. Writing Your Final Report#

A useful scientific report structure is IMRaD:

  • Introduction: What is the problem, why does it matter, and what are the research questions?
  • Methods: How was the research conducted?
  • Results: What did the experiments reveal?
  • Discussion: What do the findings mean, and what are the limitations?

Background, Related Work, and Conclusion sections may also be appropriate.

Final report evaluation checklist#

  • Does the report follow a clear scientific structure, such as IMRaD?
  • Does the text present logical, well-supported arguments?
  • Are scientific claims supported by appropriate references?
  • Is the writing precise, clear, and technically accurate?
  • Is the report divided into coherent sections with informative headings?
  • Does the Methods section explain how the research questions were addressed?
  • Are procedures detailed enough to support reproducibility?
  • Do figures and tables have informative captions or legends?
  • Are figures and tables referenced and explained in the main text?
  • Are results critically discussed rather than merely presented?
  • Are study limitations acknowledged?
  • Do conclusions address the research questions?

A strong report connects the initial problem, methodological choices, experimental evidence, and conclusions into a coherent scientific argument.

8. Final Thoughts: Taking Ownership of Your Research#

A successful project is not necessarily one in which every experiment works as expected, but one in which the scientific process is rigorous, transparent, and thoughtfully documented.

9. AI disclamer#

Please include an AI usage disclaimer if you have used any artificial intelligence (AI) tools in preparing your work.

Example:

Artificial intelligence (AI) tools, such as ChatGPT, were used to assist with language refinement, grammar correction, and content organization. All AI-generated suggestions were reviewed, edited, and verified by the author, who takes full responsibility for the accuracy, originality, and final content of this work.
